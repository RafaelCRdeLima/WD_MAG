Subject: Re: magnetized massive WDs -- congratulations, and some answers

Dear Bani, dear Zenia,

First, congratulations on the ApJL acceptance, and thank you for sending the
paper. I have read it.

Before I answer anything, let me say plainly where I stand: I am not satisfied
with our results, and we are still exploring. I am fairly sure I have not yet
included all the ingredients that matter -- our equation of state is
barotropic, there is no explicit resistivity, the MRI is unresolved, and the
one configuration that would test the most interesting case is precisely the
one we have not managed to evolve. So please read what follows as the current
state of a calculation rather than as a verdict on anything. Several of your
questions land on exactly the places where I am least comfortable, which is
why they are useful.

=== First, a correction, because the rest depends on it ===

Bani, you read the draft as saying "the magnetic field sustains only a few
seconds". That is my fault: I put it badly in an August message and the draft
has since been corrected.

What the simulation shows is that an m=1 instability grows in a few Alfven
times and removes about 98% of the magnetic ENERGY, while the field stays
ordered and stays axisymmetric. E_tor/E_pol falls from 5e5 to 4.2 during the
disruption and then climbs back to 89 by t = 78 s: the star ends up more
toroidally dominated than it was at saturation. What is lost is amplitude,
roughly a factor of six in B, not order and not geometry.

So I would not put it as "super-Chandrasekhar WDs are not magnetically
supported". The narrower statement we can actually defend is:

  a white dwarf holds at most E_tor/|W| ~ 0.0101 in a confined toroidal field
  before the magnetic pressure exceeds the gas pressure somewhere inside, at
  which point the field-free equation of state used to build the configuration
  stops describing it. Such a field is worth about +0.017 Msun.

Bani, that is your own number coming back. You wrote that 0.01 "seems to be the
bound for magnetic stability for barotropic stars". We reached 0.0101 from a
purely structural condition with no stability input, and the run then starts at
E_mag/|W| = 0.0114, marginally above, and settles two orders of magnitude
below. Whether the two coincide for a reason I genuinely do not know. A virial
argument suggests they might, since E_mag/|W| is of order the volume average of
1/beta, but I have not made that argument properly.

=== How generic, and at what resolution ===

Two meshes, 192^3 and 256^3. The disruption converges: both lose most of the
energy over the same few Alfven times. What does not converge is the level the
field settles to, the minima differing by a factor of 25 between meshes, so we
report growth rates and residues as resolution-limited and say so in the draft.

The equilibrium survey is separate and better behaved: repeating all 1200
configurations at 257^2 against 129^2 moves the ceiling by 0.01% and moves no
configuration across the beta = 1 boundary.

=== 1:1 toroidal to poloidal -- the honest state of it ===

Bani, this is the question I most want answered too, and here is exactly where
we are.

We can construct those configurations. Of 540 rotating equilibria, 40 satisfy
simultaneously: M above the Chandrasekhar mass, the star holds its own field,
comparable poloidal and toroidal energies, and T/|W| below the secular bar
threshold. The heaviest is 2.13 Msun. So your intuition that balancing the two
geometries should help is right as far as the equilibrium goes.

We have not evolved them, and the reason is specific rather than a matter of
effort. Comparable poloidal energy requires an exterior dipole near 7e11 G. The
ambient medium filling our computational box holds at most 3.4e10 G before the
field dominates its pressure, and raising the ambient to what would be needed
puts more mass in the box than there is in the star. We ran it anyway: it
aborts at t = 0.205 s from time-step collapse, at 35 times the cost per unit
physical time of the weak-dipole case. A vacuum or stratified exterior
treatment is needed and we do not have one yet.

I would rather tell you that than imply we have tested the case.

=== What drives the decay ===

Zenia, this is the sharpest question and the answer is a caveat rather than a
mechanism. Our runs are ideal MHD with no explicit resistivity, so neither Hall
nor Ohmic decay is present. What happens has two stages: the m=1 Tayler kink is
an ideal instability and cascades the ordered field to the grid scale, and what
dissipates it there is numerical resistivity. The disruption is physical; the
dissipation that follows is not controlled. We estimated what a controlled eta
with Rm ~ 10 would cost and it is about 2000^3, so this is a limitation we
state rather than one we can remove.

This bears directly on your third question. In a barotropic star there is no
threshold field for the Tayler instability: any purely toroidal field is
unstable. In a stably stratified star there is one, buoyancy stabilises weak
fields, and that is precisely the regime your dormant 1e12 G fields occupy. Our
star is barotropic by construction, so we cannot tell you where your dormant
field becomes unstable. This is one of the ingredients I believe we are
missing.

=== Does the star explode ===

Zenia, in our runs it does not. Mass is conserved to 0.05%, the star survives
the episode, and the differential rotation supporting it is never erased. But
that is because in our configuration the mass is rotational: the field carries
about 1% of the virial, so losing it costs the star very little.

In a star where the field genuinely supplies the support, losing it should be
dramatic, and your suggestion about overluminous SN Ia is the right question to
ask. The difficulty our ceiling raises sits upstream of it: a field large
enough to do the supporting is, by our measurement, larger than the star can
hold in a configuration consistent with the equation of state used to build it.
If that survives scrutiny, the trigger you are after might not need the
instability at all. I would be glad to be wrong, and the test is the
mixed-configuration evolution above.

=== The 70-90% by radius ===

Zenia, this one we can answer with a number. A twisted torus needs the toroidal
field to vanish on every field line reaching the vacuum, so it lives only in
the closed-field-line region. Measured on our equilibria:

  outer extent         0.99 R_eq   (essentially the surface)
  fraction of volume   38 - 45%    (depending on current localisation)
  fraction of mass     30 - 43%

In radial extent your 70-90% is comfortably supported, indeed exceeded. In the
fraction of the star actually carrying the field it is not: the closed region
is a torus rather than a sphere, and a sphere of 0.8 R would be 51% of the
volume against our 38-45%. Whichever of the two the phenomenology meant, those
are the numbers a higher-dimensional equilibrium gives.

=== The stochastic field and the Compton scale ===

Bani, we cannot speak to this at all. Our cell is 9.4e6 cm and the Compton
wavelength is 2.4e-10 cm, seventeen orders of magnitude apart, and the runs
carry no subgrid model. What we can report from the same runs is adjacent to
your point and is a caveat we carry: during the first twelve seconds, 49% of
sampled instants have a peak field above the Landau critical value, which is
exactly where a field-independent degenerate equation of state is least
defensible. The rotation results, measured from 12 s onward, are free of it.

=== Where the two calculations do not yet meet ===

Your B-WDs are explicitly non-rotating and approximately spherical. Our result
for non-rotating field-supported configurations is that the mass frontier
converges to the Chandrasekhar mass from below without crossing it, ending at
neutronization: the heaviest such star that holds its own field is 1.4387 Msun
against 1.44. When we add differential rotation the mass goes well past the
limit, but then the support is rotational rather than magnetic.

That is a sharp enough difference to be worth resolving rather than arguing,
and I do not assume the error is on your side. The most useful next step I can
see is the one Laura asked for and that is running as I write: the same star
without rotation, evolved to 60 s. If the ordered configuration survives
without rotation, several things above need revisiting.

The draft is on Overleaf and the conclusions are deliberately empty -- that
section should be ours rather than mine.

Thanks again to you both. These questions have already changed what I think
needs doing next.
