Dear Bani, dear Zenia,

Thank you both, these are the right questions. Let me answer them in order, and
let me start by correcting an impression the draft evidently gave, because the
rest depends on it.

=== The field does not vanish in a few seconds ===

Bani, you read it as "the magnetic field sustains only a few seconds". That is
my fault for the wording in an earlier message, and the draft has since been
corrected. What the simulation shows is this: an m=1 instability grows in a few
Alfven times and removes about 98% of the magnetic ENERGY, but the field stays
ordered and stays axisymmetric. E_tor/E_pol falls from 5e5 to 4.2 during the
disruption and then climbs back to 89 by t = 78 s -- the star ends up MORE
toroidally dominated than it was at saturation. What is lost is amplitude,
about a factor of six in B, not order and not geometry.

So the statement is not "super-Chandrasekhar WDs are not magnetically
supported". It is narrower and, I think, more useful:

  A white dwarf can hold at most E_tor/|W| ~ 0.0101 in a confined toroidal
  field before the magnetic pressure exceeds the gas pressure somewhere
  inside, at which point the field-free equation of state used to build the
  configuration no longer describes it. That field is worth about
  +0.017 Msun.

Bani, this is your own number. You wrote that 0.01 "seems to be the bound for
magnetic stability for barotropic stars". We arrived at 0.0101 from a purely
structural condition with no stability input at all, and the run then starts at
E_mag/|W| = 0.0114, marginally above, and settles two orders of magnitude
below. Whether the two coincide for a reason is open; a virial argument
suggests they might, since E_mag/|W| is of order the volume average of 1/beta.

=== How generic, and at what resolution ===

Two meshes, 192^3 and 256^3. The disruption itself converges: both lose most of
the energy over the same few Alfven times. What does NOT converge is the level
the field settles to -- the minima differ by a factor of 25 -- so we report
growth rates and residues as resolution-limited, and say so in the draft.

The equilibrium survey is separate and better converged: repeating all 1200
configurations at 257^2 against 129^2 moves the ceiling by 0.01% and moves no
configuration across the beta = 1 boundary.

=== 1:1 toroidal to poloidal: the honest state of it ===

Bani, this is the most important of your questions and I want to be plain about
where we are.

We CAN construct those configurations. In a survey of 540 rotating equilibria,
40 satisfy simultaneously: M > M_Ch, the star holds its own field, comparable
poloidal and toroidal energies, and T/|W| below the secular bar threshold. The
heaviest is 2.13 Msun. So mixed configurations above the Chandrasekhar mass
exist, and your intuition that balancing the two geometries should help is
correct as far as the equilibrium goes.

We have NOT evolved them, and the reason is specific rather than a matter of
effort. Comparable poloidal energy requires an exterior dipole near 7e11 G. The
ambient medium that fills our computational box holds at most 3.4e10 G before
the field dominates its pressure, and raising the ambient to what is needed
would put more mass in the box than there is in the star. We tried the run
anyway: it aborts at t = 0.205 s from time-step collapse, at 35 times the cost
per unit physical time of the weak-dipole case. A vacuum or stratified exterior
treatment is required and we do not have one yet.

So: the mixed configuration is constructible, not yet evolvable by us. I would
rather say that than imply we have tested it.

=== What drives the decay ===

Zenia, this is the sharpest question and the answer is a caveat rather than a
mechanism. Our runs are IDEAL MHD with no explicit resistivity, so neither Hall
nor Ohmic decay is in them. What happens is in two stages: the m=1 Tayler kink
is an ideal instability and it cascades the ordered field to the grid scale,
and what dissipates it there is numerical resistivity. The disruption is
physical; the dissipation that follows is not controlled. We estimated what it
would take to have a controlled eta with Rm ~ 10 and it is about 2000^3, so
this is a limitation we state rather than one we can remove.

This matters for your question 3. In a barotropic star there is no threshold
field for the Tayler instability -- any purely toroidal field is unstable. In a
stably stratified star there IS one, buoyancy stabilises weak fields, and that
is precisely the regime your dormant 1e12 G fields live in. Our star is
barotropic by construction, so we cannot tell you where your dormant field
becomes unstable. We can only say that by the time the field is doing the
supporting, it is well above anything a barotrope tolerates.

=== Does the star explode ===

Zenia, in our runs, no. Mass is conserved to 0.05% and the star survives the
episode intact; the differential rotation that supports it is never erased.
But that is because in OUR configuration the mass is rotational -- the field
carries about 1% of the virial, so losing it costs the star very little.

In a star where the field genuinely supplies the support, losing it should
indeed be dramatic, and your suggestion about overluminous SN Ia is the right
thing to ask. The difficulty our ceiling raises is upstream of it: a field
large enough to do the supporting is, by our measurement, larger than the star
can hold in a configuration consistent with the equation of state used to
construct it. If that holds, the trigger you are looking for may not need the
instability at all.

I would be glad to be wrong about this, and the test is the mixed-configuration
evolution above.

=== The 70-90% by radius ===

Zenia, we can answer this one with a number. A twisted torus requires the
toroidal field to vanish on every field line reaching the vacuum, so it lives
only in the closed-field-line region. Measured on our equilibria:

  outer extent            0.99 R_eq  (essentially the surface)
  fraction of volume      38 - 45%   (depending on current localisation)
  fraction of mass        30 - 43%

So in RADIAL extent your 70-90% is comfortably supported, indeed exceeded. In
fraction of the star actually carrying the field it is not: the closed region
is a torus rather than a sphere, and a sphere of 0.8 R would be 51% of the
volume against our 38-45%. Whichever of the two your phenomenology meant, the
numbers above are what a higher-dimensional equilibrium gives.

=== The stochastic field and the Compton scale ===

Bani, we cannot speak to this. Our cell is 9.4e6 cm and a Compton wavelength is
2.4e-10 cm, seventeen orders of magnitude apart, and the runs are ideal MHD
with no subgrid model. What we can report from the same runs is adjacent to
your point and is a caveat we carry: during the first twelve seconds, 49% of
sampled instants have a peak field above the Landau critical value, which is
exactly where a field-independent degenerate equation of state is least
defensible. The rotation results, measured from 12 s onward, are free of it.

=== Your paper ===

I have read it. Two things worth putting side by side rather than settling by
correspondence.

Your B-WDs are explicitly non-rotating and approximately spherical. Our result
for non-rotating, field-supported configurations is that the mass frontier
converges to M_Ch from below without crossing it, ending at neutronization: the
heaviest such star that holds its own field is 1.4387 Msun against M_Ch = 1.44.
When we add differential rotation the mass goes well past the limit, but then
the support is rotational. That is where we disagree, and it is a sharp enough
disagreement to be worth resolving rather than debating.

The most useful next step I can see is the one Laura asked for and that is
running now: the same star without rotation, evolved for 60 s. If the ordered
configuration survives without rotation, several things I have written above
need revisiting.
